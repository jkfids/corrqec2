\subsection{Quantum channels}
The physical evolution of a quantum state \(\rho \in \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\), where \(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\) is the space of bounded linear operators on a Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}\), is entirely described by a \emph{quantum channel}. A quantum channel is a linear map \(\mathcal{E}: \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{in}}) \to \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{out}})\) that is completely positive and trace preserving (CPTP). Formally, a quantum channel \(\mathcal{E}\) satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Linearity}: \(\mathcal{E}[a\rho_1+b\rho_2]=a\mathcal{E}[\rho_1]+b\mathcal{E}[\rho_2]\).
\item \textbf{Complete positivity (CP)}: \((\mathcal{I}_A\otimes \mathcal{E}_B)[\rho_{AB}]\geq 0 \quad \forall \rho_{AB}\).
\item \textbf{Trace preservation (TP)}: \(\Tr(\mathcal{E}[\rho]) = \Tr(\rho)\).
\end{enumerate}
Thus, a channel represents a \emph{two-time} quantum map evolving an initial state \(\rho(t_0)\) at time \(t_0\) to a final state \(\rho(t_1)\) at time \(t_1\), i.e., \(\mathcal{E}: \rho(t_0) \mapsto \rho(t_1)\). A quantum channel admits several equivalent representations, each useful in different contexts. The most well-known representation, the Kraus (operator-sum) representation, expresses the channel's action on an input state \(\rho\) as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E}[\rho] = \sum_i K_i \rho K_i^\dagger,
\end{equation}
where the Kraus operators \(\{K_i\}\) satisfy \(\sum_i K_i^\dagger K_i = \mathbb{I}\) to ensure trace preservation. Another important representation is the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism, which establishes a bijection between CP maps \(\mathcal{E}: \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{in}}) \to \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{out}})\) and positive semidefinite operators \(\Lambda \in \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{in}} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{out}})\). The action of the Choi matrix \(\Lambda\) is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:choi-rep}
    \mathcal{E}[\rho] = \Tr_{\mathrm{in}}[(\rho^T \otimes \mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{out}})\Lambda].
\end{equation}
The Stinespring dilation theorem provides an additional critical, physically motivated perspective. It states that any CPTP map acting on a system space \(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_S)\) can be represented as a unitary operation \(U\) on a larger, dilated space \(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_S \otimes \mathcal{H}_E)\), followed by a partial trace over the auxiliary environment space \(\mathcal{H}_E\). This is expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E}[\rho_S] = \Tr_E[U(\rho_S \otimes \sigma_E)U^\dagger].
\end{equation}
Throughout this work, we also employ other representations, such as the \(\chi\)-matrix\footnote{Also known as a process matrix, a term we avoid using due to a terminological clash with a higher-order quantum object with potentially indefinite causal order.} representation and the (Liouville-)superoperator representation. The \(\chi\)-matrix is related to the Choi matrix via a change of basis to the Pauli basis, while the superoperator representation is convenient for numerical calculations and tensor network construction. We do not explicitly cover these nor the detailed mappings between the various representations. For a comprehensive overview of quantum channel representations and the transformations between them using a graphical calculus, we refer the reader to ref.~\cite{woodtensor2015}.

\subsection{Pauli channels and twirling (WIP)}
An important class of quantum channels, particularly in quantum error correction, is the class of Pauli channels. A Pauli channel \(\mathcal{E}_P\) acting on \(n\) qubits can be expressed as a convex combination of Pauli operators
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E}_P[\rho] = \sum_{P \in \mathcal{P}^{(n)}} p_P P \rho P,
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{P}^{(n)} = \{I, X, Y, Z\}^{\otimes n}\) is the \(n\)-qubit Pauli group.

\subsection{Process tensors}
While quantum channels describe two-time dynamics, they are insufficient for capturing multi-time quantum processes, especially in the presence of non-Markovian memory effects. The quantum \emph{process tensor} provides a general spatiotemporal framework that encodes all such correlations. It can be viewed as a \emph{higher-order quantum map}, or quantum comb, that takes as input a sequence of \(k\) instruments (CP maps) \(\mathbf{A}_{0:k-1}=(\mathcal{A}_0,\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k-1})\), and outputs the resulting system state \(\rho_\mathrm{out}\). For the purposes of modelling the multi-time dynamics starting from an initial system state \(\rho_\mathrm{in}\)\footnote{For cases where the system and environment are initially correlated, the process tensor acts purely on a sequence of instruments \(\mathbf{A}_{0:k-1}\), where the initial map \(\mathcal{A}_0\) serves as a preparation map. Correspondingly, a \emph{superchannel} can be described as a 1-slot process tensor.}, we define a \emph{\(k\)-slot} process tensor \(\mathcal{T}_{0:k}\) as a multilinear map
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}_{0:k}[\rho_\mathrm{in},\mathbf{A}_{0:k-1}] = \rho_\mathrm{out}.
\end{equation}
Like quantum channels, a process tensor admits several equivalent representations, the most prevalent being the Choi representation. Just as the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism establishes a channel-state duality, there exists a process-state duality that maps a quantum process \(\mathcal{T}_{0:k}\) to a positive semidefinite operator \(\Upsilon_{0:k} \in \mathcal{B}(\bigotimes_{j=0}^k \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{in}_j} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{out}_j})\). Its action on an initial state \(\rho_{\mathrm{in}}\) and sequence \(\mathbf{A}_{0:k-1}\) is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pt-choi-rep}
    \rho_\mathrm{out}(\rho_\mathrm{in}, \mathbf{A}_{0:k-1}) = \Tr_{\overline{\mathrm{out}}} \left[\left(\rho_{\mathrm{in}}^T\otimes \hat{\mathcal{A}}_0^T\otimes \cdots \otimes\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{k-1}^T \otimes \mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{out}}\right)\Upsilon_{0:k}\right],
\end{equation}
where \(\Tr_{\overline{\mathrm{out}}}\) denotes the partial trace over all subspaces except the final output space \(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{out}_k}\), and \(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_j \in \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{out}_j} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{in}_{j+1}})\) is the Choi state of the CP map \(\mathcal{A}_j\). We remark that for \(k=0\), the above expression reduces to the quantum channel case in \cref{eq:choi-rep}. 

Equivalent Kraus, superoperator, and \(\chi\)-matrix representations can be derived from the Choi form analogously as for quantum channels. The Choi representation is convenient because it translates the physical properties of a quantum process into concrete, computable properties of the operator \(\Upsilon_{0:k}\). Crucially, a process tensor \(\mathcal{T}_{0:k}\) represents a valid, physical process if and only if its Choi representation \(\Upsilon_{0:k}\) satisfies the following conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Positive semidefiniteness.} \\
    This is the direct generalisation of the CP condition for quantum channels. The Choi matrix must be positive semidefinite:
    \begin{equation}
        \Upsilon_{0:k} \geq 0.
    \end{equation}
    This ensures that, for any valid initial state and sequence of physical instruments, the output state is also positive semidefinite.
    \item \textbf{Causal (or affine) constraints.} \\
    Also known as the containment property, these constraints are a more intricate generalisation of the TP condition to multi-time processes. The Choi state must satisfy the recursive constraints,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:affine-constraint}
        \begin{split}
            &\Tr_{\mathrm{out}_j}[\Upsilon_{0:j}] = \Upsilon_{0:j-1} \otimes \mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{in}_j}, \quad \forall j=1,\dots,k, \\
            &\Tr_{\mathrm{out}_0}[\Upsilon_{0:0}] = \mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{in}_0}.
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}
    Operationally, this condition ensures that instruments performed at future times cannot influence outcome statistics at earlier times, thereby enforcing causality. Equivalently, these constraints can be expressed as a linear system of Pauli expectation values. Denoting
    \begin{equation*}
        \braket{P_{\mathrm{in}_0}P_{\mathrm{out}_0}\cdots P_{\mathrm{in}_k}P_{\mathrm{out}_k}} = \Tr\!\left[(P_{\mathrm{in}_0}\otimes P_{\mathrm{out}_0} \otimes\cdots\otimes P_{\mathrm{in}_k}\otimes P_{\mathrm{out}_k})\,\Upsilon_{0:k}\right],
    \end{equation*}
with Pauli strings \(P_j \in \mathcal{P}^{(n)}\) of cardinality \(|\mathcal{P}^{(n)}| = 4^n\), and non-identity Pauli strings \(Q_j \in \mathcal{P}^{(n)} \setminus \{\mathbb{I}\}\), \cref{eq:affine-constraint} can be rewritten as \((k+1)(4^n-1)\) independent linear equations,
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            &\braket{P_{\mathrm{in}_0}P_{\mathrm{out}_0}\cdots P_{\mathrm{out}_{k-1}}Q_{\mathrm{in}_k}\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{out}_k}} = 0, \\
            &\braket{P_{\mathrm{in}_0}P_{\mathrm{out}_0}\cdots P_{\mathrm{out}_{k-2}}Q_{\mathrm{in}_{k-1}}\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{out}_{k-1}}\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{in}_k}\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{out}_k}} = 0, \\
            &\quad\vdots \\
            &\braket{Q_{\mathrm{in}_0}\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{out}_0}\cdots \mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{in}_k}\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{out}_k}} = 0.
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}
    The normalisation condition \(\braket{\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{in}_0}\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{out}_0}\cdots \mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{in}_k}\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{out}_k}} = \Tr{[\Upsilon_{0:k}]} = 4^{n(k+1)}\) provides an additional linear constraint, however it is often more convenient to work with the unnormalised Choi state. \\
    \Angus{These affine constraints might make sampling more efficient -- you don't need the full process tensor for times \(t > k\) if you only want samples of the first \(k\) timesteps, because all the partial traces over the latter timesteps simply give identities.}
\end{enumerate}
The set of Choi states representing physically valid quantum processes \(\{\Upsilon_{0:k}\}\) thus lies in the intersection of the convex cone of positive semidefinite operators and an affine subspace defined by the causal constraints on the process tensor.

Beyond the general validity conditions, the structural features of a process tensor reflect the physical nature of the underlying dynamics. Notably, entanglement across temporal partitions of a Choi state \(\Upsilon_{0:k}\)---referred to as \emph{temporal entanglement}---indicates that a process is \emph{genuinely quantum non-Markovian}. Concretely, a process exhibits genuinely quantum temporal correlations if and only if its Choi state cannot be written as a convex mixture of products, 
\begin{equation}
\Upsilon_{0:k} = \sum_\alpha p_\alpha \Lambda_0^\alpha \otimes \Lambda_1^\alpha \otimes \cdots \otimes \Lambda_k^\alpha,
\end{equation}
with \(p_\alpha \geq 0\), and where \(\Lambda_j^\alpha\) is the Choi representation of a CPTP channel. Conversely, a process is fully \emph{Markovian} (it exhibits neither quantum nor classical temporal correlations), if and only if its Choi state is a product across all time steps,
\begin{equation}
    \Upsilon_{0:k}^{\textrm{Markov}} = \Lambda_0 \otimes \Lambda_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \Lambda_k,
\end{equation}
where each \(\Lambda_j\) is required to be CPTP. The dynamics of a Markovian process can be equivalently generated by a sequence of independent quantum channels.

The physical origin of these structures can be traced back to the system--environment picture. By a natural extension of the Stinespring dilation theorem, any \(k\)-slot process tensor can be realised as the joint multi-time dynamics of a system \(S\) and an environment \(E\), followed by a partial trace over the environment. This is represented by the equation,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}_{0:k}[\rho_S, \mathbf{A}_{0:k-1}] = \Tr_E\left[\mathcal{U}_k \circ \mathcal{A}_{k-1} \circ \mathcal{U}_{k-1} \circ \dots \circ \mathcal{A}_0 \circ \mathcal{U}_0(\sigma_E\otimes\rho_S) \right],
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{U}(\cdot) = U(\cdot)U^\dagger\) implements a joint system--environment unitary, \(\mathbf{A}_{0:k-1}\) is a sequence of CP maps acting only on \(S\), and we have assumed an initially separable system and environment state \(\sigma_E \otimes \rho_S\).

We emphasise that the Stinespring dilation is more than a mathematical device; it depicts the fundamental physics governing open quantum dynamics. Non-Markovianity arises when the environment retains information about the system’s past and feeds it back into its future evolution. The process tensor thus compactly encapsulates all uncontrollable system--environment interactions. Operationally, non-Markovianity can be viewed as the ignorance of a larger Hilbert space, where the joint system--environment dynamics are Markovian. The dilation also provides a natural bridge to tensor network representations of process tensors, which we discuss next.

\subsection{Tensor network representation of process tensors}
Quantum maps are tensors; when correlations between their indices are sufficiently constrained, they often admit compact tensor network representations. A paradigmatic example is the matrix product state (MPS) description of quantum many-body states with area-law entanglement. The same principle extends to quantum processes: process tensors are high-dimensional objects whose temporal correlations are typically structured, making tensor network representations both natural and efficient. Accordingly, process tensors have been represented as matrix product operators (MPOs), locally purified density operators (LPDOs), and tree tensor networks (TTNs). In what follows, we present a tensor network construction of process tensors linked directly to the Stinespring dilation. Throughout, we employ abstract index notation supplemented by a graphical tensor network calculus. A detailed dictionary translating between Dirac notation, abstract indices, and graphical calculus is given in \cref{tab:notations}. 
% For vectorisation, we adopt the column-stacking convention \( X \rho Y^\dagger \longleftrightarrow (Y^* \otimes X) \dket{\rho}\).

From the Stinespring dilation, a \(k\)-slot process tensor is fully specified by a sequence of joint system--environment unitaries \(\{U_j\}_{j=0}^k\) together with an initial environment state \(\sigma_E\). For a unitary \(U\) acting on \(\mathcal{H}_E \otimes \mathcal{H}_S\) of dimensions \(d_E\) and \(d_S\), its Liouville superoperator representation is \(\mathcal{U} = U^* \otimes U\), which in index notation reads
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{U}^{e'_\out s'_\out e_\out s_\out}{}_{e'_\inp s'_\inp e_\inp s_\inp} = \overline{U}^{e'_\out s'_\out}{}_{e'_\inp s'_\inp} U^{e_\out s_\out}{}_{e_\inp s_\inp},
\end{equation*}
where \(e_\inp, s_\inp\) (\(e_\out, s_\out\)) denote the input (output) indices of the environment and system, and the prime (\('\)) marks dual (bra) indices, with unprimed indices denoting ket space. To make the connection between the Stinespring dilation and tensor networks transparent, we introduce a specialised tensor representation derived from the Liouville form via a \emph{reorder--fuse--transpose} (RFT) operation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reorder} indices to group each ket index with its corresponding bra index.
    \item \textbf{Fuse} each bra--ket (primed--unprimed) pair into a single compound index.
    \item \textbf{Transpose} globally so that time flows from left to right, consistent with standard tensor network diagrams.
\end{enumerate}
Explicitly, for a general superoperator \(\mathcal{S}\),
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{split}
    &\mathcal{S}^{a'_0 a'_1 \dots a_0 a_1}{}_{b'_0 b'_1 \dots b_0 b_1} \\
    &\qquad\qquad\xmapsto{\;\text{reorder}\;}
    \mathcal{S}^{a'_0 a_0 a'_1 a_1 \dots}{}_{b'_0 b_0 b'_1 b_1 \dots}
    \xmapsto{\;\text{fuse}\;}
    \mathcal{S}^{\alpha_0 \alpha_1 \dots}{}_{\beta_0 \beta_1 \dots}
    \xmapsto{\;\text{transpose}\;}
    \mathcal{S}^{\beta_0 \beta_1 \dots}{}_{\alpha_0 \alpha_1 \dots},
    \end{split}
\end{equation*}
where \(\alpha_j = (a'_j a_j)\) and \(\beta_j = (b'_j b_j)\) are the fused indices, with \(\dim(\alpha_j) = \dim(a_j)^2\). We use Latin letters for single indices and Greek letters for fused indices. Applying the RFT operation to a unitary superoperator \(\mathcal{U}_j\) yields the 4-legged tensor \(\mathbf{U}_{(j)}\) that serves as the building block of our network,
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{U}^{e'_{j+1} e_{j+1} b'_j b_j}{}_{e'_j e_j a'_j a_j} \xmapsto{\mathrm{RFT}} \mathbf{U}^{\mu_j \alpha_j}{}_{\mu_{j+1} \beta_j}.
\end{equation*}
Here, the fused indices are \(\mu_j = (e'_j e_j)\) for the environment, and \(\alpha_j=(a'_j a_j)\) and \(\beta_j=(b'_{j+1} b_{j+1})\) for the system input and output, respectively. Their dimensions are \(\dim(\mu_j) = d_E^2\) and \(\dim(\alpha_j) = \dim(\beta_j) = d_S^2\). The mapping is graphically depicted as
\begin{equation*}
    \valignbox{\input{figures/tensor-unitary.tikz}} = \left(\valignbox{\input{figures/unitary-to-tensor.tikz}}\right)^T,
\end{equation*}
where thick wires denote fused indices. Similarly, the initial environment tensor \(\pmb{\sigma}\) is obtained from \(\dket{\sigma_E}\) as
\begin{equation*}
    \sigma^{e'_0e_0} \xmapsto {RFT} \pmb{\sigma}_{\mu_0} 
    \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
    \valignbox{\input{figures/tensor-env-init.tikz}} = \left(\valignbox{\input{figures/vectorize-sigma.tikz}}\right)^T.
\end{equation*}
To represent the final partial trace over the environment, we include a trace tensor \(\mathbf{t}\) (the unnormalised Bell state \(\ket{\Phi^+}\) on \(E\)),
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{t}^{\mu_{k+1}} = \delta^{e'_{k+1}e_{k+1}}
    \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
    \valignbox{\input{figures/tensor-trace-env.tikz}} = \valignbox{\input{figures/trace-env.tikz}}.
\end{equation*}
The \(k\)-slot process tensor is thus constructed as a matrix product operator by contracting the environment indices \(\mu_j\) across all time steps. This yields a tensor \(\mathbf{T}\) with open system indices corresponding to the inputs (\(\alpha_j\)) and outputs (\(\beta_j\)) at each time step \(j\). In index notation,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{T}^{\alpha_0,\dots,\alpha_k}{}_{\beta_0,\dots,\beta_k} = \sum_{\mu_0,\dots,\mu_{k+1}} \pmb{\sigma}_{\mu_0}\mathbf{U}_{(0)}^{\mu_0\alpha_0}{}_{\mu_1\beta_0}\mathbf{U}_{(1)}^{\mu_1\alpha_1}{}_{\mu_2\beta_1}\cdots\mathbf{U}_{(k)}^{\mu_k\alpha_k}{}_{\mu_{k+1}\beta_k}\mathbf{t}^{\mu_{k+1}}.
\end{equation}
This construction is graphically represented as,
\begin{equation}
\valignbox{\input{figures/process-tensor.tikz}.}
\end{equation}
The only virtual indices are the environment bonds \(\mu_j\); hence the MPO bond dimension is
\(\chi = \dim{(\mu_j)} \le d_E^2\). To recover the standard Choi form, we reshape and reorder the fused indices \(\alpha_j = (a'_j, a_j)\), \(\beta_j = (b'_j, b_j)\) accordingly,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{T}^{\alpha_0,\dots,\alpha_k}{}_{\beta_0,\dots,\beta_k} \mapsto \mathbf{T}^{a_0b_0\dots a_kb_k}{}_{a'_0b'_0\dots a'_kb'_k} \longleftrightarrow \Upsilon_{0:k}.
\end{equation}
